01 Executive Brief: EA Automation Program for Global MedTech (MVP in 90 Days)
Problem Framing
[ORG_TYPE]: Global Medical Device Manufacturer (Regulated, Multi-Region) – The enterprise operates in highly regulated markets (e.g. FDA in US, TGA in AU) and across multiple regions, which amplifies complexity in its IT and business landscape. The organization maintains ~180 applications supporting ~60 top-level business capabilities, ranging from R&D and manufacturing to sales, supply chain, and post-market monitoring. Today, enterprise architecture (EA) information is scattered across Visio diagrams, spreadsheets, and tribal knowledge, making it hard to align IT investments to business strategy or respond quickly to change. Key stakeholders (CIO, Chief Enterprise Architect, platform owners, domain architects, PMO, etc.) report that architecture documentation is often outdated and not easily accessible, impeding decision-making and governance.
Current Challenges:
- Siloed & Outdated Architecture Knowledge: Business processes, application dependencies, data flows, and technology standards are documented in disparate formats. This fragmentation means architects and engineers waste time searching for information and verifying accuracy. As one EA expert noted, many organizations “fall short on EA efforts… confusing it with solution architecture… The key is focusing on lightweight processes that provide value, and automation is one way to jumpstart and improve the quality of EA”[1]. Our organization experiences similar pitfalls—manual, person-dependent EA processes that fail to keep up with the “living” nature of the enterprise.
- Slow Change and Missed Opportunities: The TOP_3_PRIORITIES are to reduce cost-to-serve, improve data product reliability, and accelerate feature delivery. However, without an integrated view of business capabilities, applications, data, and tech, identifying redundancy or high-cost areas is guesswork. For example, overlapping systems supporting the same capability remain undetected, and modernization efforts lack clear priority. 64% of EA teams in a 2023 survey said they are under-resourced[2], and most struggle to deliver strategic outcomes (only 26% fully agree EA is yielding strategic benefits[3]). Our current EA practice reflects this: it’s reactive and unable to proactively guide investment to high-impact areas (e.g. where can we cut costs without risking reliability?).
- Compliance and Risk Exposure: Operating in regulated markets means any architecture change (new system, cloud migration, process change) must comply with strict quality and security controls. Today compliance checks (against ISO 27001, FDA 21 CFR Part 820, etc.) rely on manual review late in projects, risking costly rework. There’s limited traceability from Risks (like data breaches or process failures) to mitigations in architecture. EA documents do not explicitly map Controls or policies to systems and processes, so proving compliance or assessing the impact of a new regulation is labor-intensive. This gap is dangerous in a Balanced risk stance organization where regulators expect rigorous oversight.
- Lack of Business-IT Alignment: It is not always clear how each application or technology component supports business outcomes. Enterprise architects need to show which Capabilities and Processes are enabled by which systems, and how those tie to business KPIs. According to a 2023 LeanIX report, “aligning each item in your tech stack with your business capabilities provides instant clarity as to how each software application supports your business strategy”[4]. Currently, such capability mapping is incomplete in our org, making it harder for IT to justify budgets and for business to see IT’s value. This disconnect can lead to mis-prioritized projects and uncontrolled tech sprawl, hindering the goal of faster feature velocity.
Implication: Without a change, the enterprise risks higher cost-to-serve due to duplicative or misused technology, reliability issues in data products (since data lineage and ownership aren’t clearly defined), and slow delivery as teams navigate an undocumented maze of dependencies. Moreover, regulatory compliance could be jeopardized by the inability to quickly assess the impact of changes on validated systems or control frameworks. In contrast, organizations with mature EA practices enjoy significantly better agility and confidence in decision-making – a 2023 study found top-quartile EA maturity leads to 3× higher organizational agility and nearly 3× more confidence in delivering business value (e.g. improved compliance and risk management)[5][6]. This evidence (High confidence, via a large industry survey in 2023) underscores that improving our EA practice is not just an IT initiative but a strategic imperative for the business.
Objectives & Opportunity
By launching an EA Automation Program, we aim to build an integrated, AI-augmented “digital brain” of our enterprise architecture that addresses the challenges above. In the next 90 days (MVP phase), the program will focus on:
•	Creating a Single Source of Truth: Establish a centralized EA repository (leveraging a graph database for flexibility) capturing business, application, data, and technology artifacts and their interrelationships. This will drastically reduce time spent hunting for information and ensure architecture knowledge is current. (A well-built EA repository can “save hours of manual searching across multiple platforms” and “increase agility” in responding to new business needs[7].)
•	Decision-Ready Insights: Provide dashboards and visualization of the EA repository via familiar tools (e.g. Power BI) to support leadership decisions. For instance, linking applications to business capabilities and costs will highlight candidates for rationalization, directly supporting the cost-to-serve reduction goal. Similarly, mapping data lineage across systems will help pinpoint points of failure affecting data product reliability. Real-time impact analysis (e.g. if a critical system fails or a library vulnerability is announced) will become possible via graph queries, enabling faster, fact-based decisions. Use case: when the Log4J vulnerability hit, an EA graph could quickly answer which applications and servers were impacted[8] – we seek this kind of responsiveness.
•	Faster Change with Controlled Risk: Streamline the intake of new initiatives (projects, product ideas) by automatically generating initial architecture models and compliance checklists. By embedding controls and standards into the modeling process, we “shift left” on risk management – identifying compliance gaps early. Salesforce’s EA team demonstrated this benefit: their internal EA Agent uses AI to “identify compliance gaps and suggest improvements before architects submit designs, saving significant time”[9]. We will adopt a similar approach so that every new project proposal is automatically evaluated against our security/privacy policies (e.g. tagging if PII data is involved and ensuring relevant controls are applied) and architecture standards, reducing iterative back-and-forth.
•	Aligning Execution to Strategy: Ensure that outputs of the EA program (capability maps, roadmaps, reference architectures) are actively used to drive funding and execution priorities. The EA repository will be integrated with planning processes – for example, generating a modernization roadmap that sequences projects by business value, technical dependency, and risk. This directly supports the faster feature velocity goal: by having a clear, justified roadmap, we enable swifter funding approvals and avoid analysis paralysis. Moreover, project teams can reuse EA outputs (like predefined patterns and models) rather than starting from scratch, accelerating solution design.
Ultimately, the EA automation program will serve as the connective tissue between strategy and execution, using data and AI to guide decisions. It addresses the CIO’s need for transparency (Where are we investing and why?), the Chief Architect’s need for governance (Are we compliant and following the target architecture?), and domain teams’ need for speed (What are the impact and dependencies of my change?).
Options Considered
We considered several approaches to improve enterprise architecture management:
•	Option 1: Status Quo (Manual EA Practice with Minimal Tooling). In this scenario, we would hire a few more enterprise architects and attempt to document the current and target state using traditional tools (PowerPoint/Visio diagrams, spreadsheets, perhaps a SharePoint or Confluence space to host artifacts). This option has low up-front cost and no learning curve with new tools. However, it is not sustainable for an organization of our size and complexity. The effort to manually gather and update architecture artifacts for ~180 applications and numerous business processes would be enormous, and history shows such repositories quickly become stale. The speed of business change (cloud deployments, new product features) would outpace manual updates, meaning architecture knowledge would continue to lag behind reality. This directly conflicts with our “faster velocity” priority. In short, this option fails to address the core issues of data integration, timeliness, and decision automation – not recommended.
•	Option 2: Implement an Off-the-Shelf EA Tool (e.g. LeanIX, BiZZdesign, Orbus iServer). There are mature EA management suites on the market that provide a centralized repository, meta-models aligned to frameworks (TOGAF, ArchiMate, etc.), and some built-in reporting and analytics. Adopting such a tool could jump-start our practice by providing a predefined structure and UI. However, there are trade-offs. Cost can be significant (license fees often scale with number of apps or users). Customization and Integration can also be limiting – while these tools allow some tailoring, we might struggle to fit our specific meta-model needs (for example, integrating our specific KPIs or control objectives) or to integrate with our existing developer tools and data platforms. A 2023 industry report noted that those happiest with their EA tooling were much more likely to have current and relevant deliverables[10], but achieving that typically requires the tool to adapt closely to the organization. Our concern is that a generic tool could either force us to adapt our terminology and process to fit the tool, or lead to under-utilization if it’s too complex. Moreover, vendor tools still rely heavily on manual data entry unless we invest in automation on top of them. Given our emphasis on AI augmentation and CI/CD integration, an off-the-shelf product might become a constraint. It could be a longer-term consideration (some tools have API integration that we could leverage), but for the 90-day MVP we prefer a more agile approach.
•	Option 3: Build an In-House EA Automation Framework (Recommended). This option involves developing a lightweight, tool-agnostic EA DSL (Domain-Specific Language) and using our existing platforms (Azure cloud services, Power BI, etc.) to create a custom EA management solution. The core idea is to treat our enterprise architecture data like a dataset that can be versioned, queried, and enriched with AI – rather than static diagrams. We would define a minimal meta-model (covering capabilities, processes, applications, data, etc., see Section 03 for schema) and store instances of that model in a graph database or graph-like structure. We can automate population of this repository by hooking into existing sources: e.g., import application lists from our CMDB, ingest process flows from BPMN models, “pull” data from code repositories (as suggested by Smith 2022[11]) to update technology stacks, and use CI/CD pipelines to “push” changes (like deployments, new APIs) into the EA model in real-time[12]. This dual pull/push approach ensures the EA repository is both accurate and up-to-date, with minimal manual effort. We will also integrate an AI layer: for instance, using large language models (with proper governance) to draft architecture descriptions or detect potential architecture anti-patterns by cross-checking our model against best practices. Building in-house allows tight alignment with our PRIMARY_PLATFORMS (e.g., using Microsoft Fabric for the data warehouse of EA metrics, Neo4j or Azure Cosmos for the graph, Power BI for dashboards). It also ensures we can embed our business language – customizing the meta-model, terminology, and even iconography. This customization is “widely recognized as key to ease of use and future-proofing of EA”[13]. Essentially, we design the system to fit our enterprise rather than forcing the enterprise into a generic mold. The risk here is development effort and expertise: we will need skilled architects and engineers to build and maintain the system. However, given the 90-day MVP scope, we will start small (a subset of the meta-model and a few automated data feeds) and iterate. Many organizations have successfully adopted graph-based EA repositories because of their flexibility; graph databases offer the adaptability needed to manage changing business conditions and large data volumes in EA[14]. We have high confidence that this approach will best meet our objectives of agility, integration, and innovation through AI, and we choose it as the recommended path.
Recommended Approach & Solution Overview
Solution Concept: Implement a graph-based EA knowledge hub powered by a custom DSL and augmented with AI, delivered in phases. The approach is aligned to industry standards (TOGAF/ArchiMate for structure, COBIT for governance, BPMN/UML for certain diagrams) but remains tool-agnostic and highly tailored to our needs. The outputs of this program will be consumable through familiar interfaces (Power BI dashboards, Confluence wiki pages, Git repositories) to ensure adoption by stakeholders across the organization.
Key components of the solution, corresponding to the end-to-end EA lifecycle, include:
1.	Intake & Classification: Establish a pipeline for taking in new information – whether a project brief for a new capability, a change request, or discovery of an undocumented app. We will use AI-assisted parsing to convert unstructured inputs (like a free-form project proposal or architecture diagram) into our structured DSL format. For example, a solution architect’s rough draft in Confluence can be processed by a GPT-based classifier to extract entities (applications, interfaces, data objects) and relationships, producing a draft YAML entry in the EA model (with appropriate tags for domain, business unit, etc.). Each intake will automatically assign metadata: domain (which business domain or product line it belongs to), criticality (Low/Med/High impact on business), risk flags (does it handle PII or fall under a regulatory scope), etc. This classification is crucial for downstream analysis. Acceptance criteria: 100% of new project intakes have an EA record created within the repository before architecture design begins. AI Role: Accelerate classification (e.g. NLP to tag PII, or suggest which capability a new project maps to). Governance: The EA team reviews AI-generated entries for accuracy (a “human-in-the-loop” check) before they become official.
2.	Modeling & Validation: The heart of the system is the EA DSL & graph model (see 03_DSL_and_Schema for details). As new data comes in, it is validated against schema rules: e.g., every Application must link to at least one Capability it supports, every Process must belong to a Capability, all relationships must reference valid IDs, etc. Automated validators (CLI tools and CI/CD pipeline checks) will enforce these, preventing broken links or inconsistent metadata from entering the repository. The model will capture multi-layer relationships – for instance, an “Order Fulfillment” Capability is realized by a Process which is supported by several Applications; those Applications run on certain Technology components and exchange Data Objects. With the graph, we can run queries like “show all Applications supporting Capability X and what tech they run on,” instantly. We’ll also embed control checks: for example, if a data flow connects to a system marked as containing PHI (Protected Health Information), the validator can flag absence of a required Control (like encryption or an audit log) and raise a compliance alert. This ensures our Risk & Control framework (COBIT, ISO27001) is codified in the architecture. As Orbus (2023) highlights, COBIT 5 already includes an EA governance process and aligns with TOGAF, sharing the objective of optimized risk and resource management[15][16]. Our approach leverages this synergy by mapping IT controls to architecture components (so we can easily demonstrate, for example, which systems are covered by what security policies). AI Role: Assist with model validation by reasoning over standards – e.g., an AI agent could read our security policies and then examine an architecture draft to point out omissions or deviations. (Salesforce’s EA Agent achieved a form of this multi-hop reasoning at scale, analyzing up to 100k documents to enforce standards[17][18]). Acceptance criteria: EA model passes all automated checks (or issues are flagged) on each update; >90% of architecture artifacts in the target domain are captured by end of 90 days (coverage KPI).
3.	Compilation of Artifacts: From the single source EA model, automatically generate diagrams and reports to make the information accessible:
4.	Diagrams: Use Mermaid and PlantUML scripts to render architecture views (capability maps, process flow diagrams, C4 model views of applications, etc.) directly from the underlying data. For example, given a capability and its realized applications, a Mermaid script can output a simple capability map showing relationships. This ensures consistency – if a system name changes, updating it in the DSL will update it everywhere in the diagrams. It also saves architects’ time on manual drawing. By the 90-day mark, we aim to auto-generate at least: a) a high-level Business Capability map highlighting which capabilities are under-invested (e.g. missing modern applications); b) an Application Interface diagram for a key value stream (e.g. Order-to-Cash); c) a technology stack diagram (C4 style) for a pilot area.
5.	BI Dashboards: The EA graph will be periodically exported to a relational model (e.g. Azure SQL or Fabric Lakehouse tables) to support analytics in Power BI. We will provide an out-of-the-box Power BI template that includes important metrics such as number of applications per capability, “Edges In/Out” counts (to identify heavily interfaced systems), risk coverage (ratio of risks to controls for each capability or product line), etc. The dashboard will enable drill-down, e.g., clicking on a capability shows all related applications and their key attributes (owner, costs, issues). This directly supports portfolio rationalization and transparent communication to executives. A force-directed graph visualization (using a Vega or D3 library in Power BI) will allow interactive exploration of the architecture connections, which is far more insightful than static org charts.
6.	Documents & Pages: The solution will include scripts to publish or update Confluence wiki pages and Markdown files (in a Git repo) from the EA model. For instance, an Application’s “fact sheet” page (with its description, interfaces, owner, lifecycle plan) can be generated from the DSL data. These living documents, refreshed automatically, ensure teams always refer to the latest info. We’ll also generate release notes or architecture decision records by comparing model versions: e.g., if between two versions an interface was added and a server changed, a summary can be produced listing those changes (assisting change management).
All these compiled artifacts maximize consumption of EA outputs in the tools stakeholders already use (Power BI, Confluence, Git) – meeting people where they are – thereby increasing engagement. Notably, EA maturity leaders are 2× more successful in getting stakeholders to consume EA insights than laggards[19]. By providing digestible, self-service views, we plan to drive that engagement. Acceptance criteria: At least 3 auto-generated diagram types and a functioning Power BI dashboard are delivered to stakeholders in 90 days; 80% of pilot users (architects, PMs) agree that the new visuals make it easier to understand impacts and choices (measured via quick survey).
1.	Analysis & Insights: With the model populated, we perform analyses that were either impossible or laborious before:
2.	Gap Analysis vs. Standards: The system can automatically highlight deviations from reference architectures or compliance requirements. For instance, we’ll encode reference target architecture patterns (for cloud-native apps on Azure, for data management, etc.). The EA tool can then flag applications that don’t conform (maybe an app not using the standard API Gateway or one lacking a disaster recovery setup in a regulated context). AI can help here by parsing policy documents and checking the graph for fulfillment of those controls (an approach noted by Bala (2024) – AI can “analyze internal repositories, automate routine assessments, and simulate implementation scenarios”[20]). A risk scoring can be produced for each system or capability based on these gaps, guiding where to focus remediation.
3.	Impact and Dependency Analysis: Using graph queries, we can instantly get answers to “What if” questions. For example: “If Technology X (say, a middleware service) fails or needs an upgrade, which business processes and capabilities are affected?” Or “Which applications would be impacted if we retire System Y?” This is critical for creating a modernization roadmap. We’ll perform a dependency analysis combined with business value to propose a sequenced roadmap: e.g., decouple or replace the high-risk, high-impact systems first (quickly reducing risk), and schedule lower-impact ones later. This analysis directly supports cost reduction (by identifying systems to eliminate or consolidate) and reliability (by exposing single points of failure).
4.	Redundancy & Consolidation Detection: By linking all applications to capabilities and processes, the model will reveal if multiple systems serve the same purpose. For instance, if two CRM systems are both linked to the “Customer Management” capability in different regions, it prompts an investigation: can we consolidate to one global CRM? The EA tool will include a report listing such overlaps, along with associated costs, which feeds into business cases for simplification.
5.	KPI Roll-ups: The graph can connect KPIs to the elements they measure (e.g., a “Data Product Reliability” KPI might measure incidents in certain data pipelines). By aggregating these, we produce heatmaps or health scores for each capability/domain. For example, if Capability A has 5 associated KPIs and 3 are below target, we mark that capability as needing attention. This helps leadership allocate investment (maybe Capability A needs a transformation program). Our dashboard will include a capability heatmap matrix (with axes like Criticality vs Maturity), which visually flags areas of concern in red. This approach ensures that the EA program ties back to tangible performance metrics – speaking the language of business outcomes rather than just technical metrics.
AI Role in Analysis: Beyond just querying data, AI can suggest patterns. For example, using predictive analytics, it could identify that systems built on older tech stacks are likely to cause more incidents (correlating tech age with incident records), thereby suggesting proactive upgrades – aligning with the idea that “AI’s predictive analytics can forecast technology needs and ensure effective allocation of architecture resources”[21]. It can also generate natural language summaries of architecture health for reports (e.g. “Domain X has the highest tech risk due to legacy systems accounting for 40% of its apps”). We will incorporate such capabilities in later phases; in the 90-day MVP, we’ll focus on setting up the data so these advanced insights can be added.
1.	Prioritization & Roadmapping: Using inputs from analysis (dependencies, redundancies, risk scores, business value), the EA program will facilitate creation of a Modernization Roadmap. This method will use a scoring model (like WSJF – Weighted Shortest Job First, or similar cost/value ranking) to objectively prioritize initiatives. We’ll develop templates like an Opportunity Canvas (a one-pager capturing rationale for a change: expected value, cost, and risk), and a Retire/Replace/Renovate decision tree for aging systems. Each application in the inventory will be tagged with a lifecycle disposition: e.g., “Invest” (strategic, to be scaled), “Tolerate” (keep lights on, no major investment), “Migrate” (move to new tech or platform), “Eliminate” (decommission). These decisions will be informed by data (usage stats, cost, alignment to future capabilities). The roadmap will be phased (e.g., a 12-24 month outlook) and tied to funding needs. Crucially, this planning will be dynamic – if a new high-priority regulatory requirement comes, the model can quickly reprioritize impacted items. Acceptance criteria: a draft 12-month modernization roadmap is produced by day 90 for one business domain as a pilot, which the PMO can use in the next funding cycle. The plan must show clear alignment: every proposed project on the roadmap ties to a stated business priority or risk mitigation, and these links are visible in the EA model (traceability from strategy to execution).
2.	Publishing & Socialization: The program will actively publish EA insights and engage stakeholders. This includes scheduling briefings with the CIO and business unit heads using the new capability maps and dashboards, to drive home where we need investments or policy changes. It also involves training sessions for domain architects and engineering teams on how to access and use the EA data (e.g., how to query the graph or request new analyses). We will set up webhooks or notifications – for instance, if someone updates the EA model (after an architecture board decision), relevant people get notified or a Changelog is posted. The EA team will produce monthly EA update reports highlighting key metrics (like % portfolio documented, key risks discovered, etc.) to maintain momentum and demonstrate value. As noted in the State of EA report, communicating EA’s value to the business is a top priority[22]; our approach addresses this by directly linking EA work to business outcomes (dashboards showing cost savings, risk reductions, etc., not just technical diagrams).
3.	Lifecycle Management & Governance: Finally, we will formalize EA governance to sustain this model. The operating model (see deliverable 02) defines roles such as: EA Program Manager (overseeing the process), Domain Architects (maintaining domain-specific data in the model), Platform/SRE teams (providing data inputs like deployment pipelines), PMO (ensuring projects follow EA intake), Security/Compliance (reviewing controls). A RACI matrix will clarify ownership of each step (e.g., who must approve a new architecture addition, who reviews AI suggestions, etc.). We will implement version control for the EA DSL files (likely via Git) – every change to the architecture model is logged, and major updates go through pull requests with peer review, similar to code changes. Regular Architecture Review Board (ARB) meetings (bi-weekly) will use the EA model as the basis for decisions: e.g., reviewing the impacts of proposed changes, reviewing compliance against architecture principles. The EA tool will make these reviews more efficient (the ARB can quickly visualize what’s changing and see alignment to standards). Furthermore, to manage lifecycle: we’ll have SLA for updating the model (e.g., any approved architectural change must be reflected in the EA repository within X days). We will also define “golden pathways” – standard approved patterns for common needs (like deploying a web service, or implementing data ingestion), which if followed will be auto-detected by the EA tool as compliant (whereas deviations will trigger additional scrutiny). AI can assist by monitoring the repository and alerting when an artifact is stale or a review is due (analogous to “lifecycle-aware agents flagging aging technologies” as described by Forrester’s vision of real-time EA[23]). Another AI use-case: drafting Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) or design memos based on model changes – we plan to incorporate this, with the AI generating a first draft and architects refining it.
By orchestrating these steps, the EA automation program covers the entire lifecycle: from intake of requirements, through modeling & analysis, to planning and governance, and back to refresh with each change. It will effectively create a virtuous cycle where architecture data is continuously updated and utilized, rather than created once and forgotten.
Tooling & Consumption: All outputs are designed for easy consumption: a Power BI portal for executives and PMO, a Confluence knowledge base for architects and engineers, a Git repository for version-controlled DSL files and scripts (integrated into CI/CD for quality checks), and a Neo4j browser or custom UI for power users to query the graph. This multi-channel approach ensures each stakeholder group can engage with EA in the way that best suits their needs. For example, a business leader might only view the capability heatmap and roadmap in Power BI, while an engineer might clone the EA Git repo to generate a local diagram for their application. The underlying single source of truth ensures consistency across all these views.

Risks and Mitigations
Implementing an EA automation program, especially one involving AI and new processes, comes with risks. We have identified key risks and our mitigation strategies:
•	Adoption & Cultural Resistance: There is a risk that architects or domain teams may not fully adopt the new tools and processes, sticking to old habits (e.g., keeping data in personal spreadsheets). If the repository isn’t kept up-to-date by users, its value drops. Mitigation: We involve key architects and stakeholders from day one in design (“co-create” to get buy-in). We will mandate certain processes via governance (e.g., Architecture Review Board will only consider proposals submitted via the EA intake process, enforcing usage). We also plan comprehensive training and quick wins – for example, demonstrate to each domain architect how the tool can make their job easier (maybe by auto-generating a diagram they spent hours on before). Executive sponsorship by the CIO will ensure mandate (“this is how we do architecture now”). We set KPIs like “% of projects using EA model” and monitor them. Over time, as success stories are shared (like how a team avoided an incident thanks to EA insight), momentum will build.
•	Data Quality & Completeness: The EA model could produce misleading insights if the data is inaccurate or incomplete (garbage in, garbage out). For instance, if an application’s dependencies aren’t all recorded, an impact analysis might falsely indicate safety. Mitigation: Start with a controlled scope (perhaps focus on a critical domain, like Manufacturing or Quality systems, in MVP to ensure high quality there, rather than boiling the ocean). Use automated checks to detect likely data issues (e.g., orphans: a Process with no Applications linked might indicate missing data; we flag such anomalies). Assign data stewardship roles – each domain architect “owns” the data for their domain and has an OKR to keep it updated. We’ll also implement crowdsourcing mechanisms: if someone viewing a diagram spots an inaccuracy, they can submit feedback easily (maybe a “Suggest Edit” button on Confluence pages). AI can assist by reconciling with other sources (e.g., cross-verify application lists with CMDB or cloud inventories) to highlight discrepancies. Essentially, we treat the EA data as living and introduce quality controls similar to code (reviews, testing certain assertions).
•	Scope Creep & Over-engineering: There’s a risk of trying to model everything in the first iteration or building overly complex systems that overshoot the 90-day goal. Mitigation: Strict timebox and MVP criteria – we prioritize the most important artifact types and questions to answer. For example, if our main goal is to address cost-to-serve, we might prioritize modeling cost and capability data over, say, modeling every minor infrastructure component. We will use agile sprints and showcase progress every 2 weeks to steer scope. The DSL is intentionally minimal (only ~12 core concepts) to prevent analysis paralysis in meta-model design. Additional elements can be added in later phases based on need. The 90-day plan has explicit deliverables and we will measure progress against them weekly. If something is taking too long (e.g., an overly complex AI integration), we will defer it (better to deliver a simpler working system on time).
•	AI-Related Risks (Hallucination, Bias, Compliance): Using AI (LLMs) to generate architecture content or recommendations could introduce errors – e.g., the AI might “hallucinate” a plausible-sounding architecture that is wrong, or suggest solutions that violate our policies. There are also concerns about data security if using cloud AI services (we must not expose sensitive internal info). Mitigation: We will implement AI guardrails: use AI in a “human-in-the-loop” fashion where it proposes and humans approve. For instance, an AI-generated DSL draft from a text brief must be reviewed by an architect before acceptance. We will utilize retrieval-augmented generation – meaning the AI will be provided with context from our curated knowledge (like standards) so it doesn’t invent facts, and we’ll ask it to cite sources from our internal repository for traceability. All AI usage will go through compliance review (ensuring any data sent to an AI API is scrubbed of sensitive details, or using on-premises models where needed). We’ll pilot AI suggestions on low-risk scenarios first to build trust and evaluate accuracy (with an evaluation rubric as planned in deliverable 08). Additionally, by engaging our AI Center of Excellence (if one exists, or data science team), we can ensure alignment with the company’s AI ethics guidelines. In short, we treat AI as an assistant, not an autonomous decision-maker, governed by clear policies (and we log AI decisions for audit).
•	Security & Access Control: Concentrating all EA knowledge in one system makes it a target – sensitive information (like vulnerability info, or planned decommission of a system which could be market-sensitive) must be protected. Mitigation: Implement RBAC (role-based access control) on the EA repository and dashboards. For example, maybe only certain roles can see cost figures or risk ratings. Use our existing Key Vault and identity management to secure credentials and manage access. We will also integrate the EA toolchain into our incident response plan – i.e., if a certain classification of data is present, ensure it’s encrypted at rest and in transit. Given our regulated context, we may treat the EA repository as a validated system itself, with proper change control (especially for any GMP-critical processes). Regular backups and disaster recovery for the EA data are also part of the plan (so the repository itself doesn’t become a single point of failure for decision-making).
•	Measuring Success & Continuous Improvement: It can be challenging to quantify EA’s impact, risking it being seen as overhead if not demonstrated. Mitigation: We establish clear KPIs and track them. Some we’ve noted: time-to-artifact (how quickly can we produce required architecture analysis), coverage % of applications in repository, reduction in cycle time for certain processes, etc. We will report these metrics regularly to show progress. Additionally, we gather feedback from users and iterate – the program itself will follow continuous improvement, using retrospectives to adapt the operating model or tools. By treating the EA practice like a product, with stakeholders as customers, we ensure it remains relevant and valuable.
In summary, while there are risks, our approach incorporates governance and feedback loops to manage them. This combined with the incremental rollout (MVP then extend) gives us a high confidence of delivering a useful solution with manageable risk. We note that data quality and integration are common challenges in EA and AI projects[27], so we will pay special attention there. The project’s risk profile is medium, but with strong executive support and cross-functional collaboration, the upside far outweighs the potential downsides.
90-Day Implementation Plan (MVP Roadmap)
We propose an aggressive but achievable timeline to deliver the MVP in 90 days, divided into three roughly 30-day phases with key milestones:
•	Days 1–30: Foundation & Quick Wins
•	Setup & Kickoff: Form the core team, finalize scope and success metrics. Conduct a kickoff with key stakeholders (CIO, chief architect, domain leads) to set expectations and communicate the vision (ensuring everyone understands the why).
•	Meta-model & Data Gathering: Develop the initial EA DSL schema (node types, relationships, required fields) – see deliverable 03 for the finalized schema. This involves reviewing existing frameworks (TOGAF content meta-model, ArchiMate, etc.) and selecting the minimal set of concepts needed. In parallel, inventory critical data sources: e.g., get the list of all applications from IT asset systems, gather existing process documentation for one pilot domain (say Supply Chain), list known capabilities from strategy documents.
•	Infrastructure Setup: Stand up the graph database environment (e.g., create a Neo4j instance or equivalent). Set up a Git repository for DSL files and automation scripts, and a basic CI pipeline (with linting and schema validation on commit). Also establish a Power BI workspace for EA and ensure necessary connectors (to the graph or exported data) are in place.
•	Pilot Ingestion & Manual Modeling: Take one high-priority business capability (for example, “Order Fulfillment” or “Regulatory Compliance”) and manually model it in the DSL with the team to flesh out the approach. This yields a concrete example and surfaces any schema adjustments needed. If available, use AI to assist (e.g., feed an existing architecture doc to the prompt and have it draft YAML – using this to refine our prompt templates). By end of month 1, we should have a working draft model for the pilot domain (perhaps 10-15 applications, 5-10 processes, etc., captured) and initial relationship mappings. Also deliver one quick-win visualization: e.g., a simple capability map or app-dependency graph for that domain, to demonstrate value to stakeholders early. (This is crucial to build momentum – show something tangible in 30 days.)
•	Days 31–60: Build Core Features & Integrations
•	Automation Development: Implement the pipelines for Pull and Push data. For pull: for instance, write a script to parse our source code repositories for dependency info (as described by Smith, a nightly job scanning pom.xml or package.json to update tech dependencies[28]). For push: integrate with our CI/CD so that whenever a new service is deployed or an infrastructure change happens (perhaps via an Azure DevOps pipeline), it triggers an update to the EA model (maybe via an API or message). We’ll likely start small, e.g., instrument one pipeline to send data to the EA system as a proof of concept.
•	AI Integration (prototype): Develop a few-shot prompt and policy for one use-case – e.g., “Requirement → DSL draft”. This could be a script where a user story or brief is input and an OpenAI API (or Azure OpenAI if using internal) returns a proposed YAML snippet. Put guardrails (like using a predefined template and asking the model to fill slots, rather than freeform). Begin testing this with real examples. Also, implement an AI-based gap checker: e.g., a prompt that asks “does this architecture meet our defined standards X, Y, Z?” using our standards documentation as context. This might use a retrieval QA approach. By day 60, we aim to have at least one AI-assisted function working (even if rudimentary) – for instance, a command-line tool where you paste text and get DSL output with some accuracy.
•	Expand Data Model & Reconcile: Import more data into the EA model – cover all critical applications and their basic info (owner, lifecycle, etc.), at least at a coarse level. Reconcile with teams: hold workshops with domain experts to verify the data (e.g., ensure no major system or interface is missed in the pilot domain model). Continue to refine the schema as needed (maybe we realized we need a new field or relation – incorporate it).
•	Visualization & Dashboard Build: Now that more data is in place, build out the Power BI dashboard with connections to the data. Create visuals for the agreed KPIs (e.g., application count by capability, risk heatmap, etc.). Implement the Vega-lite force graph if possible to show relationships interactively. Also set up the template for capability heatmap matrix (even if manually populated at first). Additionally, generate Mermaid/PlantUML diagrams for 2-3 different views: e.g., application interface diagram and a process flow. This will likely involve writing scripts that query the graph and output the syntax for these diagrams (deliverable 06 would contain these scripts; for MVP, we might do some manual step, but aim to automate one fully).
•	Governance Establishment: Draft the Lifecycle Playbook (deliverable 05 outline) – basically, define how versions are managed, how often data is refreshed (perhaps designate a day of the month for full review?), and roles for upkeep. Socialize a RACI chart and get agreement especially from Security and PMO on their involvement (e.g., Security must sign-off that controls in the EA model meet their requirements; PMO must include EA checkpoints in project lifecycle). Possibly conduct the first Architecture Review Board meeting using the new model to validate it in action.
•	Days 61–90: Pilot Deployment & Handover
•	Pilot Execution: By this phase, we should run an end-to-end pilot of the new process on a real project or domain. For example, take an upcoming initiative (like “Implement IoT monitoring for devices”) and go through intake (enter the idea, generate DSL, validate), model (architect adds missing details), analysis (see impacts, compliance), and planning (see where it fits in roadmap). Use the outputs (dashboards, diagrams) in the project’s kickoff or business case. Essentially, battle-test the system with real usage. Gather feedback from the team involved – did it save time, what was confusing, etc. Use that to make final tweaks.
•	Training & Enablement: Develop training materials (could be short video demos or a how-to Confluence page). Conduct training sessions for architects, engineers, and product managers on how to use the new EA tools: searching the repository, reading the dashboards, requesting changes, etc. Also train the EA team itself on maintaining the system (how to update the schema, how to run the AI tools, etc. for sustainability).
•	Finalize Documentation: Complete all deliverables for the MVP: the Executive Brief (this document) to communicate to executives; the Operating Model document detailing processes; the DSL schema documentation (with examples and mappings) for reference; the Roadmap method guide, Playbook, etc. Even if some are in draft, ensure they’re in a shareable state. This acts as both deliverables and the basis for scaling beyond MVP.
•	Value Review and Next Steps: At day ~90, hold a stakeholder review meeting (steering committee). Present the outcomes: demonstrate the live system (show a query or dashboard live), report on KPIs achieved (e.g., “we documented X% of target systems, identified Y redundancy opportunities, initial analysis suggests \$Z potential savings”), and capture testimonials from pilot users. Compare to baseline (did we meet the success criteria we set?). Then propose the next steps: a 12-month roadmap for rolling this out enterprise-wide, including resource needs if any. Typically, next steps may include onboarding additional domains (manufacturing, R&D, etc. sequentially), deeper AI integrations (like automated roadmap suggestions), integration with other systems (e.g., ITSM or incident management integration to feed into EA model), etc. Also address long-term ownership: likely propose that the EA Office continues to own the program, with possibly a dedicated EA platform architect role. If all goes well, secure executive agreement to continue the program into that next phase (with funding if needed).
This 90-day plan is intense but realistic given it focuses on a slice of the enterprise (not everything at once) and leverages many existing assets (we’re not building everything from scratch – we use proven open-source libraries, existing data, etc.). Similar initiatives have been done in short timeframes; for example, Salesforce stood up their internal EA AI agent in about three months by focusing on a narrow scope and working closely with governance from the start[29]. We will emulate that agile, security-by-design approach. Each phase above has clear exit criteria and deliverables, which will keep us on track. The plan also builds in early demonstration of value (end of month 1) to maintain sponsorship interest through to completion.
